## Summary

F1: ARFC 0003 gains section 3.9, Process handover. Each key travels with its counters and windows. The successor marks a session consumed before it first uses it, and a resuming old process discards every consumed session, reserves a new handshake counter block and re-handshakes. ARFC 0017 section 6 states the matching step, and ADR 0057 records the decision.
F2: ARFC 0003 section 3.6 seals probe messages under the newest confirmed session. At a rekey the responder stays on the previous session until the initiator uses the new one, and probe receive state of the previous session is kept until the peer is seen on the newer one, at most 180 s.

Check against I-07 and ARFC 0020 section 3.2, case by case, for one node's sessions across a handover (the reviewer's "handover-fail" case is case 3):

1. Successful handover: every key moves with its counters and windows (3.9, "State that travels"), and the old process never uses the session again ("Exclusive use"). This is one counter sequence and one window per key, as without a handover.
2. Failed handover, successor never used session S: S is not consumed, and the old process resumes S. Nobody sealed or accepted under S after the old process passed it, so its counters and windows are current. No nonce repeats and no message passes twice.
3. Failed handover, successor sealed or accepted under S: S is consumed, because the record comes before first use. The old process erases S and never uses it again, so it cannot reuse a nonce or accept a replay under S's keys. Any later session has fresh keys (3.3).
4. Successor crashes after recording and before first use: S is discarded with no use. That costs one handshake, which is safe.
5. Both processes live at once: the old process resumes only after the successor has ended (3.9), so no session has two users.
6. Successor rekeyed S, or answered the peer's handshake: it marked S consumed before sending the handshake message. The old process discards S and re-handshakes, so it does not keep a session the peer has moved off.
7. Handshake counter: the old process reserves a new block from the persisted counter (3.2). The new block is above every value either process reserved, so the peer's 3.2 check does not drop its new initiation.

Check of F2 against the reviewer's required outcome, at a rekey where N is the responder and P the initiator:

- Response lost: P's newest session is still the previous one, P is not confirmed on the new one, and N seals under the previous one. Both hold it, so probing works until P retries.
- Response delivered, P silent: P holds the previous `probe_recv_key` until it accepts a probe message from N under a newer session. N stays on the previous one until P uses the new one. Both hold the previous one, at most 180 s after its handshake.
- P uses the new session: N accepts that message and the session is confirmed. N switches, and P drops the previous probe state at N's first probe message under the new session.

## Decisions

- The handover rule is a new section, ARFC 0003 3.9, and not text inside 3.4 and 3.6. It covers transport, probes and relay envelopes alike. 3.4 and 3.6 refer to it. Build and verify tasks cite `arfc-0003#3.9`.
- 3.3 is edited by two sentences. Previous-session probe keys may outlive the 10 s previous receive key, never past 180 s. Without this, the F2 rule contradicts 3.3's "each probe key has the lifetime of the session key of the same direction". Tasks built on the 3.3 key lifetimes need recutting for this.
- F2 uses confirmation plus retention of the previous probe state, not a timer. The task's own rule (the responder waits for the initiator to use the new session) breaks when P is silent for more than the 10 s previous receive key. A responder timer breaks when the response is lost and P retries late, and 3.1 sets no retry time.
- The successor also marks a session consumed before sending a handshake message to its peer. Without it, an old process could resume a session the peer had already replaced.
- The resuming old process reserves a new handshake counter block. Without it, its first initiations could repeat counters the successor sent and be dropped by the peer (3.2).
